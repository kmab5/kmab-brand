# examples

rewrites and annotated passages. the rules in `WRITING_STYLE.md` are abstract;
this is what they look like when they hit the page.

---

## 1. project blurb — medium register

**AI draft**

> ## Introducing Gebeta: A Modern Take on a Timeless Classic
>
> Gebeta is a seamless, browser-based reimagining of the beloved Ethiopian
> mancala tradition. Built with performance in mind, it delivers an intuitive
> experience that honors the game's rich heritage — while leveraging modern web
> technologies to make it accessible to players everywhere.
>
> Ready to play? Let's dive in.

<!-- kmab-writing: ignore-start -->

what's wrong: Title Case heading. "seamless", "leveraging", "rich heritage",
"intuitive". three-adjective rhythm. em dash as glue. call-to-action ending.
nothing concrete; you finish it not knowing what the game does.

<!-- kmab-writing: ignore-end -->

**rewrite**

> ### gebeta
>
> A browser rendition of the Ethiopian game (mancala) with a rules engine, turn
> logic, and just enough AI to make losing interesting.

lowercase heading, one sentence, three actual things it contains, and the
deflating joke at the end doing the work that "engaging gameplay" was pretending
to do.

## 2. blog opener — informal register

**AI draft**

> ## Lessons From Competitive Mathematics: What Olympiad Training Taught Me About Debugging
>
> In the world of software engineering, debugging is often seen as a necessary
> evil. But what if I told you that the skills required to solve complex
> mathematical problems translate directly to the art of finding bugs?
>
> Let's explore three key insights.

<!-- kmab-writing: ignore-start -->

what's wrong: Title Case, subtitle-colon headline, "in the world of", "what if I
told you", "necessary evil", "the art of", numbered promise up front.

<!-- kmab-writing: ignore-end -->

**the actual post**

> # sitting with a hard problem.
>
> olympiad training and exams doesn't really teach you tricks. the exam questions
> dont have a pattern. you dont have a data structure or algorithm that you
> learned in DSA class that conveniently fits each question. no, it teaches you to
> stay in the room with a problem long after it stops being comfortable, to keep
> turning it over when the obvious moves are gone.
>
> i've found that that habit turns out to be most of debugging.

lowercase title with a trailing period. opens by saying what the thing *isn't*
in concrete terms, three sentences building, then the flat one-line landing.
`dont` and `i've` in the same paragraph: inconsistent apostrophes, correct
register.

## 3. a hard truth — informal register

**AI draft**

> It's important to set realistic expectations. While transfer applications can
> be challenging, with careful preparation and persistence, many students find
> success. Remember: every journey is unique!

what's wrong: hedged into meaninglessness, uniformly encouraging, exclamation
mark on a platitude, "every journey is unique."

**the actual guide**

> one thing to note, the chances of the transfer actually pulling off is very
> low. i dont say this to discourage ppl or anything but the reality of the fact
> is that unis dont want to do bureaucratic work with a scholarship student who
> wont pay them anything in the middle of an already established class.

says the discouraging thing first, then explains the mechanism. the reader is
trusted with the bad news.

## 4. formal register done right

formal doesn't mean the vocabulary goes up. it means the ambiguity goes down.

**wrong kind of formal**

> Applicants are strongly encouraged to leverage all available resources in order
> to facilitate an optimal outcome with respect to their transfer endeavours.

**right kind of formal**

> Universities in Türkiye commonly announce yatay geçiş applications after
> spring-semester grades have been finalized. The main application period is
> often late June, July, or early August.

same voice as the informal guide. shorthand gone, apostrophes complete, jokes
removed, because this page gets read in a second language by someone with a
deadline. the sentences are still short and still say a specific thing.

## 5. annotated: the parenthetical undercut

the most reusable move in the voice. main clause makes the claim, parenthesis
takes the air out.

> Who's Sami? (I insist people use Sami over Samuel; too professional)

sets up an identity essay, immediately refuses to be pompous about it. note the
semicolon inside the parenthesis; the punctuation is doing timing work.

> He would be concerned that my silence made me unsociable (he would be wrong).

four words, disagrees with his own father, moves on.

> im very into f1. (yeah, we figured, not like the entire site didnt give it away)

answers the reader's eye-roll before the reader can have it.

**how to use it:** write the plain sentence. then ask what an unimpressed friend
reading over your shoulder would say. that's the parenthesis.

## 6. annotated: the deflating close

endings don't summarize and don't recruit. they let the air out.

> I finally sit down to eat the delicious food my lovely mother cooked, still
> bamboozled by the fact that Duny was Emperor Emhyr. Turns out, I still have a
> lot of learning to do.

after a full essay on bookmarks and self-knowledge, the last beat is a plot
twist in a fantasy novel.

> This is Sami (not Samuel). And he is not complete yet.

> A pressing worry of mine is the dire health portents accompanying writing,
> namely hunched backs, crooked fingers, and huge glasses.

an essay about the love of writing ends on posture.

> that last principle is why adding a blog was easy: it's just another folder of
> markdown the engine knows how to render. get ready for a *lot* of blogs.

**how to use it:** whatever the piece just claimed, end on the smallest, most
ordinary, most self-implicating detail available.

## 7. annotated: sentence rhythm

> It tasted like poison. My eyes were going back and forth between the glass of
> tomato juice — one sip from full — I was holding in my hand and my mom, who was
> holding a slipper to throw at me if I wasn't going to finish it. Tears trickled
> down my cheeks as I gulped down the bitter juice and showed her the empty glass.
> She had said it'd cure the nauseating anemia I was experiencing, but at the
> moment, I couldn't think of that. I hated tomatoes.

four words. forty. twenty-three. twenty-five. three.

the short ones are the argument; the long ones are the evidence. the em dashes
in sentence two are a genuine interruption — the parenthetical detail `one sip
from full` is what makes the glass real — and they don't appear again anywhere
in the paragraph.

## 8. common repairs

| symptom | fix |
| --- | --- |
| opens with why the topic matters | delete the paragraph. start at the first concrete thing. |
| every sentence 18–24 words | break three of them into fragments. |
| em dash in most paragraphs | keep the one that's a real interruption. commas and full stops for the rest. |
| three adjectives in a row | pick one, or swap all three for a number. |
| ends on "so go build something" | end on the smallest embarrassing detail instead. |
| Title Case heading | lowercase it. |
| big word doing no work | ask: joke, or exact? if neither, plain word. |
| uniformly positive throughout | find the thing that's actually annoying and say so. |
| bullets all identical length | let two of them be lumpy. |
